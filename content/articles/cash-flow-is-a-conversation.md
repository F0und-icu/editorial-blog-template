# Cash flow is a conversation.

A profitable company can run short of cash. A company reporting a weak quarter can generate substantial cash by collecting old invoices or reducing inventory. Neither observation automatically proves that the accounting is wrong. Profit and cash answer different questions about the same business.

To make the distinction concrete, consider a fictional distributor called Northbank Supply. The name, business, and financial figures in this essay are invented. All amounts are in millions of dollars. We use a simplified annual cash-flow bridge, leaving out taxes payable, acquisitions, leases, and other items that a real filing may require.

## Begin with the bridge

Northbank reports net income of $12 million. Depreciation is $3 million, reflecting a non-cash allocation of earlier asset spending. Accounts receivable increase by $5 million, inventory increases by $2 million, and accounts payable increase by $1 million.

The simplified bridge from profit to cash generated by operations is therefore $12 + $3 − $5 − $2 + $1 = $9 million. The company then spends $4 million on property and equipment, leaving $5 million under a simple operating-cash-flow-minus-capital-expenditure definition of free cash flow.

| Item | Cash-flow adjustment |
| --- | ---: |
| Net income | $12m |
| Add depreciation | +$3m |
| Increase in receivables | −$5m |
| Increase in inventory | −$2m |
| Increase in payables | +$1m |
| Operating cash flow | $9m |
| Capital expenditure | −$4m |
| Illustrative free cash flow | $5m |

## Ask why receivables grew

A receivable is a sale for which cash has not yet been collected. Growth in receivables can accompany healthy expansion. It can also reflect slower collections, more generous payment terms, a shift in customer mix, or revenue recognized before a customer's ability to pay is clear.

A single year's change is not enough to distinguish these explanations. Compare receivable growth with revenue growth, examine the aging schedule, and read the policy for estimating credit losses. If sales are seasonal, a year-end balance may be unusually flattering or unusually severe.

Days sales outstanding can help structure the question, but it is not a verdict. An average receivables balance divided by credit sales, multiplied by the number of days in the period, is more informative than treating one closing balance as representative of the entire year.

## Inventory holds both options and costs

More inventory can improve availability and protect against a supply interruption. It also ties up cash, takes space, and may become obsolete. A business selling spare parts might rationally hold items for much longer than a grocery distributor. Comparing their inventory turns without understanding the product would produce a tidy but misleading ranking.

For Northbank, the $2 million inventory increase is a use of cash in this period. Whether it is a good use depends on the reason for the purchase and the likely selling price. The accounting adjustment tells us where the cash went; operating context helps explain whether it will come back.

## Capital expenditure is not one category of decision

Free cash flow is a useful shorthand, but definitions vary. Some spending replaces worn equipment. Some expands capacity. Some meets regulatory requirements. A management estimate of “maintenance capex” can be informative, yet it is not a universally standardized accounting number.

An analyst should reconcile any adjusted cash-flow measure to the statements and ask which costs have been excluded. If a supposedly exceptional expense appears every year, excluding it may make a business look permanently less expensive to operate than it really is.

> A cash-flow statement is most useful when it starts a conversation with the income statement, balance sheet, and notes.

## Use several periods and read the footnotes

One period can be distorted by collection timing, supplier negotiations, or a large order received just before the reporting date. A multi-year view helps reveal whether growth regularly consumes cash and whether that consumption earns an adequate return.

A practical reading sequence is to reconcile profit to operating cash flow, identify the largest working-capital movements, compare capital spending with the asset base, and then read the relevant accounting notes. Only after those steps should a valuation multiple carry much weight.

The [SEC's guide to financial statements](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/investorpubsbegfinstmtguide) offers an introduction to the statements and their relationships. Northbank is not a security available for purchase, and the bridge is an educational example rather than a claim about any listed company's accounts.
