# A yield is not a total return.

A quoted yield can be a helpful starting point, but the label deserves a definition. A distribution yield, an earnings yield, and a bond's yield to maturity are not the same measurement. They use different numerators, different assumptions, and sometimes different time periods.

## Follow both income and price

In a simple hypothetical example, an asset purchased for $100 pays $5 during the year and ends the year at $92. Ignoring reinvestment, costs, and taxes, its holding-period total return is ($5 + $92 − $100) / $100, or −3%. Receiving income did not prevent a loss in overall value.

The reverse is possible too. An asset can pay little current income and still appreciate, although that appreciation is uncertain and does not pay a cash obligation until it is realized or financed against.

## Name the convention

Before comparing two income figures, write down whether each is trailing or forward-looking, gross or net of expenses, and annualized from a shorter period. Ask whether a distribution includes a return of capital. A high payout is not automatically evidence of high economic earnings.

This is an educational example, not a recommendation for an income strategy. The useful habit is simple: read the yield's definition, then ask which components of the total outcome remain outside it.
